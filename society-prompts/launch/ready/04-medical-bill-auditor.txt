=== THE PERSON ===

Gwen, 52, [CITY]. Four days in the hospital in March. In June a bill arrived for $4,812.40
after insurance. It is one page. It says things like "PHARMACY $1,204.00" and "MED SUPPLIES
$611.55" and "ROOM & BOARD 4 DA $1,890.00." There is no way to tell what any of it was.

She has called twice. The first time she was on hold 40 minutes and was told to call
billing on Tuesday. The second time she was told the amount was correct. She has no idea
whether it is, and neither does the person who told her.

She is not going to become a medical billing expert. She has about two hours of energy for
this, total, spread over a month.

=== THE PROBLEM, WITH A NUMBER ===

The summary bill Gwen received is not the bill. It's a rollup. The itemized statement — the
one with dates, codes, quantities, and unit prices — is what makes errors visible, and
patients generally have to ask for it specifically. Step one of this app is not analysis; it
is getting her the actual document.

Do not quote an error-rate statistic in the UI unless you can source it to a named study with
a year. Advocacy groups cite figures ranging widely and most trace back to unpublished vendor
claims. If you cannot verify one, display none.

=== WHAT SUCCESS LOOKS LIKE ===

Gwen ends up with three things: an itemized bill she can read, a list of specific line items
worth questioning phrased as questions rather than accusations, and a signed financial
assistance application in an envelope. Any one of the three is a win.

=== BUILD THIS ===

Six parts, in this order. Parts 1 and 6 matter more than the analysis engine — build them
first and last respectively, and do not let the parser eat the schedule.

1. GET THE REAL BILL — First screen asks one question: "Do you have the itemized bill, or
   just the summary?" If summary (the common case), generate a request letter and phone
   script immediately, before any upload:
   - A letter requesting a fully itemized statement with dates of service, CPT/HCPCS codes,
     revenue codes, quantities, and unit charges
   - A phone script with the exact words to say and what to write down (name, date, time,
     reference number)
   - A one-line note that requesting it does not waive anything and does not restart any clock
   Also, always: tell her to request the medical records for the same dates, because
   comparing charges to the chart is how you find services that never happened.

2. UPLOAD + PARSE — Accept PDF (text or scanned), photos of paper bills, and CSV. Extract a
   table of line items: date of service, code, code type, description, quantity, unit charge,
   total charge, insurance paid, adjustment, patient responsibility.
   Parsing hospital bills is genuinely hard and hospital formats are all different. Design for
   that honestly:
   - Show the parsed table beside the source page image, side by side, always.
   - Every extracted field is editable. Assume you got some wrong.
   - Show a per-row confidence and highlight low-confidence rows for review.
   - If parsing fails entirely, fall back to a manual entry grid. This is not a failure
     state; for many bills it will be the primary path. Make it fast: tab between cells,
     paste from a spreadsheet, autocomplete on repeated codes.
   - OCR for scans: run locally in the browser (tesseract.js) rather than uploading to a
     service. See SAFETY.

3. THE CHECKS — Run these, and only these. Each finding must name the specific rows involved,
   explain in one plain sentence what it might mean, and state clearly that it might be
   legitimate. Never assert fraud. Never assert overcharge.
   a. DUPLICATE LINES — same code, same date, same quantity, appearing more than once.
   b. DATE-RANGE VIOLATIONS — charges dated before admission or after discharge.
   c. QUANTITY OUTLIERS — a quantity implausible for the code and stay length (e.g. 6 chest
      X-rays in one day, 30 units of a once-daily drug). Define the thresholds in a data file
      with reasoning, not in code.
   d. UNBUNDLING — code pairs that CMS's National Correct Coding Initiative (NCCI)
      Procedure-to-Procedure edit tables say should not be billed together. These edit files
      are published quarterly by CMS and are free to download. Load them as data; cite the
      quarter and version in every finding.
   e. ROOM-DAY ARITHMETIC — room and board days billed versus stated admission and discharge
      dates. Note the standard convention that the discharge day is typically not charged, and
      flag mismatches as a question, not an error.
   f. UNSPECIFIED LINES — "MISC," "SUPPLIES," "OTHER" with no code. These are legitimate to
      question and easy for a patient to ask about.
   g. GENDER/AGE-INCOMPATIBLE CODES — where the code set marks a code as sex- or age-specific
      and it conflicts with what the patient entered. Only if you can source the flags.
   h. PRICE COMPARISON — see part 4.
   Every check produces a finding object with: severity, affected rows, plain-language
   explanation, the exact question to ask billing, and the source of the rule.

4. PRICE CONTEXT — Compare charged amounts against published references:
   - The hospital's own machine-readable standard charges file. Federal hospital price
     transparency rules require hospitals to publish these; they are commonly at
     /cms-hpt.txt-referenced locations or a "price transparency" page on the hospital site.
     Formats vary in practice despite the standardized schema. Build an importer that accepts
     the file, and if you cannot locate or parse it, say so on the screen rather than
     substituting an estimate.
   - Medicare rates as a floor-of-reference: the Medicare Physician Fee Schedule and hospital
     outpatient/inpatient rates are published by CMS.
   Present comparisons carefully: "This hospital's own posted cash price for this code is $X;
   you were charged $Y." Never say "you were overcharged." A charge above Medicare is normal
   and expected; the useful signal is a charge above the hospital's own published price.
   If you cannot verify a reference price, show nothing. An invented benchmark here does real
   damage.

5. THE LETTERS — Generate, editable, printable, with the patient's data filled in:
   a. Itemized bill request (part 1)
   b. Billing inquiry — lists each finding as a numbered question, requests a written
      response within 30 days, states that the patient is disputing pending clarification
   c. Financial assistance / charity care application cover letter. Nonprofit hospitals are
      required under section 501(r) of the Internal Revenue Code to have a written financial
      assistance policy and to limit amounts charged to eligible patients. Look up the
      specific hospital's policy, link it, and tell the patient what income threshold applies
      and what to attach. This is frequently the single highest-value action in the entire
      app — a bill can go to zero — so surface it early and prominently, not buried at the end.
   d. Insurance appeal — internal appeal letter citing the denial reason from the EOB, with a
      note about the external review right and the deadline. Verify deadlines for [STATE].
   e. No Surprises Act inquiry — if the visit was emergency care, or care at an in-network
      facility from an out-of-network provider, generate a letter raising the federal balance
      billing protections and the patient-provider dispute resolution process for uninsured
      and self-pay patients. Verify the current process and deadlines before citing them.

6. WHAT TO DO NEXT — A short ordered plan, personalized by what was found, with realistic
   time estimates and what to expect. Include: [STATE]'s consumer assistance program or
   insurance department complaint line, the CFPB complaint process, and the current state of
   medical debt credit reporting. That last one has changed repeatedly and has been subject
   to litigation — VERIFY the current status before writing a sentence about it, and if you
   cannot, write only "Rules about medical debt and credit reports have changed recently;
   check with [State consumer assistance program]."

=== LICENSING — READ THIS BEFORE SHIPPING ANY CODE SET ===

This will constrain your design and an agent that skips it will build something
undistributable.

- CPT codes and their descriptions are copyrighted by the American Medical Association and
  require a license to redistribute. You may let a user type a CPT code they received on
  their own bill and reason about it. You MUST NOT ship a bundled CPT code-to-description
  lookup table without a license. Do not scrape one.
- HCPCS Level II codes are published by CMS and are in the public domain — you may ship those.
- ICD-10-CM is published by CDC/CMS and may be redistributed.
- NCCI edit files are published by CMS and may be used.
- NUBC revenue codes are licensed by the American Hospital Association; check before bundling.
Verify each of these statements against the current primary source before relying on it, and
record what you verified in a LICENSING.md file in the repo. If a data source turns out to be
unlicensable, degrade gracefully: show the code the user entered without a description rather
than shipping data you cannot distribute.

=== STACK ===

- Local-first: React + TypeScript + Vite, PWA, IndexedDB. NO SERVER for patient data.
- PDF text extraction: pdf.js. OCR: tesseract.js, in a web worker, in the browser.
- Table extraction: write a heuristic layout parser over pdf.js text items using x-coordinate
  clustering. Do not reach for a cloud document AI service — see SAFETY.
- Reference data (NCCI edits, HCPCS, Medicare rates) ships as static compressed JSON built by
  a script in /scripts/fetch-reference-data.ts that records source URL, version, and fetch
  date for each. That script is the thing a maintainer runs quarterly.
- PDF letter output: pdf-lib, client-side.
- Under 250KB JS gzipped excluding the OCR worker and reference data, both lazy-loaded only
  when needed.

=== HARD CONSTRAINTS ===

- No medical data leaves the device. Ever. This is the central architectural constraint:
  every parse, every check, every letter, generated client-side. Show this claim on the home
  screen and make it verifiable — a test must assert zero third-party network requests during
  a complete case, including OCR.
- The tool must work with the network off after first load.
- Every finding must be phrased as a question to ask, never as a conclusion. Write a test that
  greps generated output for "overcharge," "fraud," "illegal," "they owe you," "error" used
  as an assertion, and fails the build.
- Every finding cites its rule source and version. A finding without a source is a bug.
- WCAG 2.2 AA. This user base skews older; default body text 18px minimum, and the parsed
  table must remain usable at 200% zoom (horizontal scroll within the table region only,
  never the page).
- Reading level 6th grade for all app copy. The letters are formal — flag that difference to
  the user in one line.
- [LANGUAGES] from the first commit.

=== DO NOT BUILD ===

- No account, no cloud storage, no "upload your bill and we'll review it" backend.
- No LLM in the checking path. Every finding must be traceable to a deterministic rule and a
  cited source. An LLM may be used at build time to draft plain-language copy for a human to
  review; it must not run at request time on a patient's bill.
- No negotiation-on-your-behalf, no contingency fee, no "we'll handle it" service layer, no
  referral to a paid bill-negotiation company. Those exist, they take a cut, and building a
  funnel to them betrays the user.
- No price estimator for future procedures. Different problem, different data quality.
- No provider ratings, no hospital scorecards, no public database of charges.
- No claim submission, no clearinghouse integration, no EDI.
- Do not attempt to detect upcoding or medical necessity. Both require clinical judgment and
  the chart. Out of scope; say so in the app.

=== ACCEPTANCE TESTS ===

1. A complete case — upload, parse, check, generate all letters — produces zero network
   requests to any third-party origin, including during OCR.
2. Duplicate detection flags two identical code+date+quantity rows and does not flag two rows
   with the same code on different dates.
3. A charge dated one day after the recorded discharge date is flagged; a charge on the
   discharge date is not.
4. An NCCI-listed PTP code pair on the same date produces an unbundling finding citing the
   NCCI file version.
5. Every finding object has a non-empty rule_source and rule_version.
6. No generated letter or UI string contains an accusatory assertion (grep test).
7. Editing a parsed row updates the findings without re-running OCR.
8. A bill that fails to parse falls back to the manual grid with no data loss and no error
   dialog dead-end.
9. Pasting a 40-row block from a spreadsheet into the manual grid populates correctly.
10. The financial assistance letter appears in the recommended actions for any case where the
    user indicated income below the threshold they entered, regardless of what the checks found.
11. Reference price comparison renders "no published price available" when the hospital file
    is absent, and never substitutes an estimate.
12. The app functions fully offline.
13. Stored data contains no full date of birth, no SSN, no member ID beyond what the user
    typed, and no address.
14. Clearing the case deletes all blobs, verified by inspecting IndexedDB.

=== SAFETY + LEGAL ===

- Not medical, legal, or financial advice. Persistent footer, and a line in every letter:
  "I am asking these questions as a patient reviewing my own bill."
- Never assert an error. Every finding is a question. This is both an ethical line and a
  practical one: a patient who walks into billing accusing them of fraud gets less help than
  one who asks nine specific questions.
- The no-server design is a health-privacy decision. Bills reveal diagnoses. A server holding
  them is a breach target and potentially a HIPAA-adjacent obligation you do not want and
  cannot meet. Do not add a backend "just for support tickets."
- OCR must be local for the same reason. Do not call a cloud vision API on a document that
  names someone's medical procedures.
- Deadlines matter and vary: insurance internal appeals, external review, and No Surprises Act
  dispute processes each have their own clock. Where you display a deadline, cite the source
  and the date you verified it. Where you cannot verify one, say "check your plan documents
  for the deadline" rather than guessing. A missed appeal deadline caused by a wrong number in
  your app is the worst outcome this project can produce.
- Add a visible "When to stop and get help" section: if the bill exceeds a few thousand
  dollars, if collections has started, or if a lawsuit has been filed, link to legal aid in
  [CITY] and the [STATE] consumer assistance program. Some situations need a human, and the
  app should know its own limits and say so.
