=== THE PEOPLE ===

Dana is 52. Her son uses opioids. She has thought about getting naloxone for a year and hasn't,
because she doesn't know where to get it, assumes she needs a prescription, and is afraid of
what the pharmacist will think of her.

She needs three facts: naloxone is available over the counter, her state almost certainly has a
standing order and free distribution, and nobody at the counter will ask her why.

The second person is Ellis, 24, who uses alone in a room and knows perfectly well that this is
the risk. Nobody has told him that a hotline exists where a person stays on the phone with you
and calls for help if you stop responding.

The third is Ray, 19, who is in a room where someone has stopped breathing and is deciding
whether calling 911 will get him arrested. Most states have a law that protects him. He has never
heard of it, and the seconds he spends deciding are the ones that matter.

=== THE FRAMING ===

This is a public health tool. Naloxone reverses opioid overdose, it has no potential for misuse,
it does nothing to a person who has not taken opioids, and access to it is endorsed across the
medical and public health mainstream. Build it in that register.

Three rules for every line of copy:

1. **No moralizing, ever.** Not "sadly," not "struggling with," not "clean," not "abuse," not
   "addict." Use "person who uses drugs," "overdose," "substance use." Write a blocklist and fail
   the build on it. Stigma is the mechanism that keeps naloxone out of the houses that need it.
2. **Never lecture about stopping.** The app's job is to keep someone alive today. Treatment
   information belongs in the app, offered plainly, never as a condition or a nudge attached to
   the life-saving content.
3. **Address the bystander as competent.** Dana, Ellis, and Ray can follow instructions. Give
   them the instructions.

=== THE PROBLEM ===

Look up current overdose mortality figures from CDC for [STATE] and cite them with a year. If you
cannot verify a figure, display none.

The gaps this addresses are practical, not attitudinal:
- Naloxone is available over the counter in the US — FDA approved an OTC naloxone nasal spray in
  2023 — and many people still believe it requires a prescription. Verify current status.
- Most states have standing orders, pharmacy access laws, and free distribution through health
  departments and harm reduction programs. Verify [STATE]'s specifics.
- Most states have Good Samaritan laws giving some protection to people who call 911 for an
  overdose. **The protections are partial and they vary a great deal.** Verify [STATE]'s exact
  scope and limits and state them precisely — overstating them is dangerous, and understating
  them costs a call.

=== WHAT SUCCESS LOOKS LIKE ===

Dana walks into a pharmacy and asks for naloxone without rehearsing a speech. Ellis has a number
saved. Ray calls 911 in the first ten seconds.

=== BUILD THIS ===

1. WHAT TO DO RIGHT NOW (/) — The default screen. Assume the reader is in the room, frightened,
   and reading on a phone. Largest type on the page, no scrolling to reach the first action,
   works offline, one tap from a cold start.
   Quote the response steps verbatim from an authoritative source — SAMHSA, CDC, or your state
   health department — with a citation. Do not compose your own clinical instructions. The
   sequence generally covers: check responsiveness, call 911, give naloxone, support breathing,
   put them on their side, stay until help arrives, and give another dose if there is no response
   after a few minutes.
   Then, immediately below and in the same weight: what [STATE]'s Good Samaritan law does and
   does not protect, in two sentences, with the citation. Ray's decision is happening right now.

2. GET NALOXONE — The directory, and the reason to build this at all.
   - **Free sources first**, because cost and pharmacy anxiety are the two barriers: [COUNTY]
     health department distribution, harm reduction programs, syringe services programs, mail
     programs (several states and national organizations mail naloxone free — research and verify
     which serve [STATE]), community distribution events, and any vending machines or public
     supply boxes, which a growing number of places now have.
   - **Pharmacies**: which ones stock it, that no prescription is needed, roughly what OTC costs,
     and whether insurance or Medicaid covers it in [STATE]. Include the sentence to say at the
     counter, because that is the actual barrier: "I'd like to buy naloxone." Nothing else is
     required.
   - Each entry: address, hours, phone, cost, whether ID is required (usually not — say so
     explicitly), languages, and a verified_on date. Phone-verify every one.
   - Note which forms are available (nasal spray vs injectable) and that nasal spray needs no
     training beyond reading the box.

3. HOW TO USE IT — Illustrated, plain, quoted from the manufacturer's instructions and an
   authoritative public health source, with citations. Include what to expect afterward: naloxone
   can precipitate withdrawal, which is unpleasant and not dangerous, and the person may be
   frightened or angry when they come around; it wears off before many opioids do, which is why
   911 still matters even if they wake up.
   Printable one-page card in [LANGUAGES], large type, for a fridge, a wallet, or a supply box.

4. IF YOU USE ALONE — For Ellis. Plainly, without judgment:
   - Never-use-alone phone lines, where an operator stays with you and calls for help if you stop
     responding. Verify the current national number and any [STATE] line, and confirm they are
     operating by calling.
   - Fentanyl test strips: what they do, what they cannot tell you (they do not measure how much,
     and a negative does not mean safe), where to get them, and — verify this carefully —
     whether they are legal in [STATE]. Several states have decriminalized them and several
     still treat them as drug paraphernalia. State [STATE]'s current law with its citation, and
     if it is unclear, say so rather than guessing.
   - Practical risk reduction, sourced from a public health authority and not authored by you:
     not using alone, going slower after any break in use, not mixing with alcohol or
     benzodiazepines, and keeping naloxone within reach of where you are rather than in a drawer.

5. GOOD SAMARITAN LAW — Its own page, because it is the single fact most likely to change what
   happens in the room. Verify [STATE]'s statute and state precisely: who is protected, what they
   are protected from, what is explicitly not covered (in many states protection is narrow —
   possession but not distribution, and often nothing about probation, parole, or warrants), and
   what it means for the person overdosing versus the person who calls.
   Never overstate this. Give people the real shape of the protection and let them decide with
   accurate information; a promise that turns out to be false costs someone their liberty and
   costs the next person their trust in the whole page.

6. AFTER — Offered, never pushed:
   - Getting more naloxone, because it was just used
   - SAMHSA's national helpline and [STATE]'s treatment locator, with a plain line that
     treatment is available when and if they want it and that this page will still be here if
     they do not
   - Medication for opioid use disorder: what it is, that it is a mainstream medical treatment,
     and where to get it in [COUNTY]. Verify low-barrier and same-day options, which exist in
     more places than people expect.
   - Grief and bereavement support for families
   - 988 for anyone in crisis
