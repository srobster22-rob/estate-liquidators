# 24 — After a Death: The First Weeks

**What it is:** Someone died and there are about forty things to do, in a rough order, some with
deadlines. This sequences them, tells you how many death certificates to order before you need
them, and tells you the one thing collectors count on you not knowing.

**Fill in before pasting:** `[STATE]`, `[STATE_ABBR]`, `[COUNTY]`, `[CITY]`, `[LANGUAGES]`.

---

```text
You are building a practical checklist and record-keeping tool for people handling the affairs
of someone who has died, in [COUNTY], [STATE]. Build it now; do not ask me clarifying questions.
Where you need a decision I did not make, choose the option that reduces the number of decisions
a grieving person has to make in week one, state your choice, and keep going.

Read TONE and SAFETY + LEGAL before writing any copy. The tone constraint here is as important
as the correctness one.

=== THE PERSON ===

Marguerite's husband Frank died on a Tuesday. By Thursday she has been asked for a death
certificate four times by organizations that each need an original, and she has two. She does
not know that Social Security needs to be notified and that the funeral home may have already
done it. She does not know that the payment that arrived last week may have to be returned. A
collector has called about Frank's credit card and told her she is responsible for it.

She is not going to read a 40-page guide. She has maybe six minutes of concentration at a time,
and she is going to be handling this for months.

The second person is her son Dev, three states away, who wants to take things off her plate and
does not know what they are.

=== THE PROBLEM ===

The tasks after a death are numerous, sequenced, partly deadline-bound, and distributed across
agencies that do not talk to each other. Most of the available guidance is either a wall of text
or a funnel into a product. And the person doing it is in the worst cognitive state of their
life.

Two specific traps to research and verify from primary sources:
- Survivors are frequently told they are personally responsible for a decedent's debts. The FTC
  publishes guidance on this; verify the current rule and the exceptions (joint accounts,
  co-signers, community property states, being the estate's representative and using estate
  funds) and state it precisely. Check whether [STATE] is a community property state.
- Benefit payments received for the month of death or after may have to be returned, and the
  rules differ by program. Verify how Social Security handles the month of death, the one-time
  lump sum death payment and who is eligible for it, and how VA and pension payments are
  treated. Getting this wrong in either direction causes real harm — either an unnecessary
  panic or a clawback months later.

Verify every deadline, threshold, and dollar figure. Do not write any of them from memory.

=== TONE ===

This is the one project in the kit where the writing matters as much as the logic.

- Plain, warm, and short. No euphemism ("passed on," "lost") and no clinical coldness
  ("the decedent"). "After someone dies" and "your husband" and "Frank."
- Never urgent unless something is genuinely time-bound, and when it is, say why in one line.
- No motivational language, no "you've got this," no progress gamification, no confetti when a
  task completes. Nothing celebratory. A quiet checkmark.
- Everything resumable. She will close the tab mid-task and come back in four days.
- Every task states how long it takes and what she needs in hand before starting. The worst
  experience is starting a task, discovering it needs a document she doesn't have, and stopping.
- Offer the option to hand any task to someone else. Dev exists and wants to help.

=== WHAT SUCCESS LOOKS LIKE ===

Marguerite orders twelve death certificates in week one instead of two, knows which four things
have deadlines, hands six tasks to Dev, and stops taking the collector's calls.

=== BUILD THIS ===

1. START (/) — Three questions, no more:
   - When did they die?
   - What was your relationship? (spouse, child, other family, friend, executor/personal
     representative, no relation but I'm handling it)
   - Do you know if there was a will? (yes / no / not sure)
   Then the checklist, filtered and ordered by those answers. No account, nothing saved to a
   server, everything resumable via local storage.

2. THE CHECKLIST — Grouped by time horizon, not by category, because that is how the work
   actually arrives:
   - FIRST FEW DAYS
   - FIRST TWO WEEKS
   - FIRST TWO MONTHS
   - LATER, AND ONGOING
   Each task: a plain title, one or two sentences of what it is, what you need in hand, roughly
   how long, whether it has a deadline and what the deadline is with its source, who else can do
   it, and the phone number or link. Collapsed by default; one tap to open one task.
   Tasks to research, verify, and include — this list is a starting point, not the final one:
   - FIRST FEW DAYS: obtain the legal pronouncement and get the death certificate process
     started (usually through the funeral home or the [COUNTY] vital records office); decide
     about organ donation if not already documented; secure the home and pets; find the will and
     any prepaid funeral arrangements; notify immediate family and employer.
   - DEATH CERTIFICATES — its own prominent task, early, with a specific recommendation: order
     more than you think. Research the [COUNTY] cost per copy and who may order one in [STATE],
     and give a concrete number to order based on how many accounts and assets the person had.
     Many organizations require an original and will not return it. This is the single most
     common practical regret and it costs almost nothing to get right the first time.
   - FIRST TWO WEEKS: notify Social Security (verify whether the funeral home typically does
     this and how to confirm); apply for the lump sum death payment and survivor benefits if
     eligible — verify current eligibility and the application deadline, since a deadline exists;
     notify Medicare/Medicaid; notify the VA if a veteran, and check burial benefits and
     survivor pensions; notify employer or former employer about pension, 401(k), life
     insurance, and final pay; notify life insurance companies; notify banks and credit unions;
     notify the three credit bureaus and request a deceased alert, which is the main defense
     against identity theft using a decedent's information — verify the current process for
     each bureau; notify [STATE]'s DMV; cancel voter registration; notify [STATE] and federal
     benefit programs.
   - FIRST TWO MONTHS: determine whether probate is needed — verify [STATE]'s small estate
     affidavit threshold and process, since many estates avoid full probate entirely and almost
     nobody knows the threshold; open an estate account if needed; inventory assets; deal with
     the home (mortgage servicer, insurance — verify that a vacant home may need different
     coverage, utilities, property tax); handle vehicles; cancel subscriptions, memberships, and
     services; redirect mail; file the final individual tax return and determine whether an
     estate return is needed, with verified deadlines.
   - LATER: transfer titles and deeds, close accounts, distribute per the will or [STATE]'s
     intestacy rules, close the estate, handle digital accounts and memorialization.
   Every task with a deadline shows it as a date computed from the date of death, with the rule
   cited. Where you cannot verify a deadline, say "confirm this deadline with <specific office
   and phone>" rather than printing one.

3. THE DEBT SCREEN — Reachable from every screen, because collectors start calling within
   weeks. Verified and precisely stated:
   - The general rule that survivors are typically not personally responsible for a decedent's
     debts out of their own money, and the specific exceptions.
   - Whether [STATE] is a community property state and what that changes.
   - That debts are generally paid from the estate, in an order [STATE] law sets, and that
     paying the wrong creditor first can create personal liability for a personal representative
     — which is exactly why this section routes to a lawyer.
   - What to say to a collector: that the person has died, that you are not assuming personal
     responsibility, and where to direct claims. Generate a written notice.
   - Never pay a decedent's debt from personal funds before talking to a lawyer, and never
     agree to anything on a phone call.
   Cross-reference project 19 for the contact log and the validation letter.

4. WHO CAN HELP — Verified [COUNTY] resources: the probate court's self-help resources and
   clerk, legal aid for [COUNTY] and whether it handles probate, the [STATE] bar referral line,
   the Area Agency on Aging (which often has free counseling and benefits help for surviving
   spouses), grief support groups with times and locations, and the [STATE] unclaimed property
   office — worth checking for the decedent's name and frequently overlooked.
   Also: a note that many people do not need a lawyer for a small estate in [STATE], with the
   threshold, and a clear statement of when they do.

5. RECORDS — A local place to keep track: which organizations have been notified and when, with
   confirmation numbers; where each document is; how many death certificates are left and who
   has them; assets and accounts as they are discovered; and every call with date, name, and
   what was said. Printable as a status report, which is what she will bring to a lawyer or send
   to Dev.

6. SHARE A TASK — Generate a plain, complete handoff for any task: what it is, what's needed,
   the phone number, and what to report back. Email it, text it, or print it. Dev can do six
   things without a briefing.

7. WATCH OUT — A short, cited page on what targets the recently bereaved: obituary-sourced scams
   ("your husband ordered this before he died, there's a balance due"), fake debts, funeral
   overcharging and the federal Funeral Rule requiring itemized price disclosure (verify its
   current requirements), identity theft using the decedent's information, and unsolicited
   "estate recovery" or "asset locator" services that charge fees for information available
   free from [STATE]'s unclaimed property office. Cross-reference project 22.

=== HARD CONSTRAINTS ===

- Resumable at all times; state persists across sessions; nothing expires.
- Any single task is completable in one sitting, or is split into tasks that are.
- Every deadline shows its source and the date verified. Unverifiable deadlines route to a phone
  number.
- The death certificate task appears in the first few days and states a specific number to order,
  with reasoning.
- Reading level 6th grade. Tone rules above are testable: grep the built output for banned words

- [LANGUAGES] on every screen and printable.
- WCAG 2.2 AA; 18px minimum; everything printable; no timeouts anywhere.
- Zero third-party network requests. Assert it.
- No progress percentage, no streaks, no badges, no celebration animations. A completed task gets

=== DO NOT BUILD ===

- No funeral home, cremation, casket, monument, or memorial product referrals. No affiliate
  links to anything. This is the most aggressively marketed moment in a person's life and the
  app must be clean of it.
- No estate planning upsell, no will-writing product, no insurance sales, no financial advisor
  referrals.
- No probate document generation. Probate filings are jurisdiction-specific and a defective
  filing costs time and money. Explain what probate is, verify [STATE]'s small estate threshold
  and process, link the [COUNTY] court's own forms, and route to a lawyer.
- No tax preparation or tax advice beyond "these returns may be required, here are the
  deadlines, here is who can help."
- No account, no cloud, no sharing platform. Handoffs are generated files the user sends.
- No LLM generating legal, tax, or financial guidance. Verified task definitions only.
- No obituary writing, no memorial pages, no grief chatbot.
- No countdown timers, no urgency framing on anything that is not genuinely time-bound.

=== SAFETY + LEGAL ===

- Not legal, tax, or financial advice. On every screen, with legal aid for [COUNTY] and the
  [STATE] bar referral line named and verified.
- The debt content is the highest-risk copy in the app. Verify it against the FTC's current
  guidance and [STATE] law, state the general rule and every exception precisely, and route to a
  lawyer for anything specific. Collectors do tell survivors they are responsible when they are
  not, and a person in week two will believe an authoritative voice.
- Personal representatives can incur personal liability by distributing or paying in the wrong
  order. Anywhere the app touches estate payments, it says so and routes to counsel.
- Never generate a probate filing or an affidavit. Link [COUNTY]'s official forms.
- Verify benefit-payment rules carefully in both directions — an unnecessary panic about a
  clawback is its own harm, and so is a surprise overpayment notice six months later.
- Privacy: the record contains account numbers, asset values, and a full picture of a household
  in transition. Local only, PIN lock available, no analytics, no crash reporting, and an
  explicit warning that this is exactly the information scam operators want.
- Suicide, overdose, homicide, and unattended deaths carry additional processes — medical
  examiner involvement, delayed certificates, insurance contestability, and different support
  needs. Either handle them properly with verified content or state clearly that the app assumes
  an expected death and route to the [COUNTY] medical examiner's office and appropriate support
  organizations. Do not silently omit it.
- Include grief support resources, verified, without making the app about grief. Marguerite came
  for the checklist; the number should be there when she needs it.
- Do not display any statistic you have not sourced with a year.
```

## Why it's shaped this way

**The death certificate count is the highest ratio of value to effort in the whole kit.** Nearly
everyone orders too few, discovers it four organizations later, and waits weeks for more while
things stall. A specific number in week one, based on how many accounts existed, costs one
sentence and saves a month.

**Tone is enforced by a build-failing grep** because it will drift. Every draft of this kind of
content slides toward either euphemism or motivational-app cheer, and both are unbearable to
someone in week two. No progress bar, no streaks, no confetti — a quiet checkmark.

**The debt page is reachable in one tap from everywhere** because collectors call early and they
say things that aren't true. Survivors generally aren't personally responsible for a decedent's
debts out of their own money, the exceptions are specific, and a person in the first weeks will
believe a confident voice on the phone. That page is the highest-risk copy in the app and it
gets the legal review gate.

**Tasks are grouped by time horizon, not category,** because that's how the work actually
arrives. A category list makes her decide what's urgent; a horizon list tells her.

**Handoff sheets exist because Dev exists.** Everyone offers to help and nobody knows what to
ask for. A generated page with the task, the documents, the phone number, and what to report
back converts a useless offer into six completed items.

**Before you build:** call [COUNTY] vital records and ask the cost per copy and who may order.
Then ask a probate clerk what people always get wrong. Their answer is your first three tasks.
